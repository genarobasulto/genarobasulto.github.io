Job Market Paper
Algorithmic Ranking and Delivery Foreclosure in Retail Platforms: Evidence from Amazon and Mercado Libre
Latest version

Retail platforms act as gatekeepers between consumers and sellers through ranking algorithms, and many vertically integrate into delivery services such as Fulfilled by Amazon, creating an incentive to favor sellers who adopt these services in rankings. This paper estimates how this practice affects prices, delivery adoption, and welfare, separating algorithmic bias from consumers' valuation of integrated delivery, and shows how both ranking regulation and competition between platforms constrain self-preferencing. Using a novel panel of more than 415,000 product-seller-time observations from Amazon and Mercado Libre, Mexico's two largest retail platforms, I estimate a structural model of consumers' platform choice and search with frictions, platforms' ranking and delivery-pricing decisions, and sellers' pricing and delivery choices. Integrated delivery is higher quality than third-party alternatives but about 84% more expensive. Products using FBA or Full receive systematically better rankings: FBA moves products up about 4 positions on Amazon and Full about 6 positions on Mercado Libre relative to comparable products using third-party delivery. Counterfactual simulations show that preventing platforms from favoring their integrated delivery services in rankings reduces integrated delivery adoption by roughly 5% on both platforms and lowers prices, generating a 4.56% increase in total surplus in the full platform equilibrium. The effects are asymmetric: Amazon's consumer surplus rises by 12.09% and its market share by 18.60%, while Mercado Libre's consumer surplus rises by 2.10% and its market share falls by 14.34%. A second counterfactual shows that competition itself disciplines self-preferencing: removing Mercado Libre's competitive constraint lowers total surplus by 4.43% and Amazon consumer surplus by 16.72%.

Working Papers

Platforms as Search Technologies: Seller Selection and Price Spillovers

Joint with Aniko Öry
Latest version

We study a market in which producers sell their products both through a platform and through a direct channel, and set prices on both channels simultaneously. Off-platform, consumers engage in costly sequential search. The platform improves consumer-seller matching but weakens price competition by concentrating consumer attention. Consumers decide whether to join based on the expected utility gain from improved matching, while sellers decide whether to list based on the incremental profit from platform sales net of diverted off-platform demand. We study platform fee design and then extend the framework to competition between two symmetric platforms, highlighting tensions between participation, pricing, producer surplus, and consumer welfare.

Keynesian Search in Stochastic Overlapping Generations Models

Joint with Parand Akbari, Eunsik Kim, and Stephen Spear Revise & Resubmit — American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics
Latest version

We study how beliefs about future wealth are disciplined in a stochastic overlapping generations economy with incomplete labor markets and Keynesian search in the tradition of Roger Farmer. We show that rational expectations must reproduce the endogenous wealth distribution, disciplining an otherwise indeterminate steady state. We calibrate a 58-period lifecycle economy with dual labor markets and matching-efficiency shocks, solve it using a neural network, and recover equilibrium expectations.

Strategic Pricing and Adoption Dynamics for Hydrogen Mobility Technologies

Joint with Alan Montgomery and Aniko Öry
Latest version

This paper develops a dynamic model of technology adoption under uncertainty and network effects, focusing on how a hydrogen supplier decides when to enter markets and how to price and design contracts for heterogeneous trucking customers. We calibrate the model using New York Advanced Clean Fleets reporting data and Bizminer industry profiles of New York trucking operators. We use the model to study subsidies for station construction and customer adoption costs and to compare personalized contracts with single-price benchmarks.
